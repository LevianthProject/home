## Context

Many parental-control products focus on blocking access, limiting time, or monitoring activity. ML Space explored a different relationship: educational activity becomes an understandable path toward approved screen time, with parents seeing the full loop.

## The core loop

1. A parent creates or selects an educational activity.
2. The child completes the activity.
3. The system validates completion.
4. The child receives approved reward time.
5. The parent monitors learning progress and usage.

Activities could include questions, e-books, educational videos, learning challenges, and custom parent-created content.

## Product system

Potential product areas included screen-time and activity monitoring, educational tasks, question building, reward-time rules, parent and child dashboards, teacher and class dashboards, challenges, avatars, themes, streaks, family accounts, school licensing, smart-device integration, and premium content creation.

The system needed to feel collaborative without hiding the adult’s responsibility for boundaries and safety.

## My product focus

- Define the parent–child interaction model.
- Design the educational reward loop.
- Structure multiple account and dashboard types.
- Explore subscription and institutional licensing models.
- Connect education, gamification, monitoring, and trust.
- Prevent the product from feeling purely punitive.

## Key decisions

### Make the exchange visible

Children should understand why reward time is available, what activity created it, and what happens next. Parents should see the same relationship rather than operating a hidden points system.

### Treat education as activity, not one content format

The product direction allowed questions, video, reading, and parent-created tasks so the loop could adapt to different learning contexts without implying one activity was always superior.

### Separate household and institution contexts

Parent–child use and teacher–student use share some mechanics but operate under different authority, privacy, and licensing expectations. Their dashboards and permissions cannot be collapsed into one generic role.

## Progress and last-known status

The last documented status placed development at approximately 70 percent. That number is presented only as a historical progress marker and needs current validation before any live-product claim.

The strongest portfolio evidence is the behavioral model: screen time reframed as a transparent exchange between learning, trust, boundaries, and reward.

## Trade-offs

Gamification can motivate, but it can also create point-chasing or conflict if rules feel arbitrary. Monitoring can support parents, but it also introduces privacy and trust responsibilities. Expanding into schools may improve reach while multiplying role, consent, and data-governance requirements.

## What I learned

Changing a product’s emotional model can matter more than adding another control. The key direction was not “more parental restrictions”; it was a clearer relationship between effort, agreement, reward, and visibility.
